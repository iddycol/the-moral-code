# MC-08 Provenance — Prefer repair to revenge

## Current rule

> When harm occurs: acknowledge it, stop it, protect against repetition, restore what can be restored, compensate where appropriate, learn from it, and rehabilitate where possible. Dangerous people may need to be separated from others, but the purpose is protection, correction and restoration — not hatred.

## Provisional verdict

**Retain strongly, but sharpen the distinction between accountability and vengeance. Do not turn forgiveness or reconciliation into duties imposed on victims.**

The Code's deepest claim is not that every wrong can be repaired or that punishment is unnecessary. It is that **the deliberate production of suffering should not become an objective in itself**.

A defensible response to wrongdoing can include restraint, loss of liberty, public condemnation, restitution and proportionate punishment where needed for protection, accountability and justice. It should reject cruelty, humiliation and revenge as ends.

## Convergence and support

### Buddhist punishment — strong anti-vengeance support

The Stanford Encyclopedia's discussion of Nāgārjuna describes punishment as a regrettable necessity that should not arise from anger or revenge. It may serve restraint, deterrence, rehabilitation and reform, and should be as mild as is consistent with protecting society. The offender remains within the scope of compassion.

Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, *Ethics in Indian Buddhism*: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/

This closely matches the current Moral Code distinction between secure restraint and dehumanising punishment.

### Restorative justice — strong modern support, not a universal substitute

UNODC describes restorative justice as a participatory, problem-solving approach that can complement or sometimes provide an alternative to conventional justice. Its focus includes repairing harm, giving victims a voice, encouraging responsibility, making amends and reducing recurrence.

Sources:
- UNODC, *Handbook on Restorative Justice Programmes*: https://www.unodc.org/documents/justice-and-prison-reform/20-01146_Handbook_on_Restorative_Justice_Programmes.pdf
- UNODC, *Introductory Handbook on the Prevention of Recidivism and the Social Reintegration of Offenders*: https://www.unodc.org/documents/justice-and-prison-reform/18-02303_ebook.pdf

Crucial qualification: restorative processes are not one-size-fits-all and cannot erase the need for protection, due process or proportional accountability.

### Christian reconciliation traditions — support with an important victim safeguard

Contemporary scholarship on Christian reconciliation connects justice, peace, mercy, acknowledgment, reparations, apology, restorative punishment and forgiveness. Scholarship on restorative justice explicitly warns that forgiveness can contribute to restoration but **must not be required**.

Sources:
- Daniel Philpott, *Just and Unjust Peace*: https://academic.oup.com/book/12078
- John Braithwaite, *Redeeming the 'F' Word in Restorative Justice*: https://academic.oup.com/ojlr/article-abstract/5/1/79/1752340
- Stephen J. Pope, *The Role of Forgiveness in Reconciliation and Restorative Justice*: https://academic.oup.com/book/5031/chapter-abstract/147549357

Project consequence: a system must never coerce a victim into forgiveness to make the offender's rehabilitation administratively convenient.

## Important distinctions

### Repair is not pretending the wrong did not happen

Repair begins with truthful acknowledgment. It may require restitution, compensation, institutional reform and records that preserve responsibility.

### Mercy is not release regardless of risk

A person who remains seriously dangerous may need secure separation. The morally relevant question is what restriction is necessary to protect others, not how much additional suffering can be imposed.

### Forgiveness belongs to the person wronged

A court, government, community or AI cannot manufacture personal forgiveness on a victim's behalf. Public legal consequences and personal forgiveness are separate questions.

### Some harms are irreparable

Death, severe trauma and destroyed communities cannot simply be "restored". In those cases the rule requires prevention of recurrence, truthful acknowledgment, appropriate compensation/reparation and humane accountability rather than pretending restoration is complete.

## Proposed v0.2 amendment candidate

Possible title:

> **MC-08 — Repair harm; reject vengeance**

Candidate text:

> **When wrongdoing occurs, first stop continuing harm and protect those at risk. Establish responsibility through fair evidence and due process, acknowledge the harm, restore or compensate what can reasonably be restored, and reduce the chance of recurrence through reform or rehabilitation where possible. Restrictions and punishment may be necessary for protection and accountability, but humiliation, cruelty and suffering for their own sake are not legitimate objectives. Forgiveness and reconciliation may be valuable, but they cannot be demanded from victims.**

## Crucible requirements discovered

- violent offender who remains dangerous;
- remorseful offender posing low future risk;
- victim pressured to reconcile;
- state torture framed as deserved punishment;
- corporate wrongdoing requiring restitution and structural reform;
- post-conflict amnesty versus accountability;
- wrongful conviction and state reparation;
- and an AI recommending harsher punishment because public anger is high.

## Confidence

**Very high** that rejection of vengeance/cruelty as an objective belongs in the core.

**High** that accountability, victim voice and protection must be made more explicit so "restoration" does not become sentimental or coercive.
