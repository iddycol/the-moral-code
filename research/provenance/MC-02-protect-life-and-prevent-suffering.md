# MC-02 Provenance — Protect life and prevent suffering

## Current rule

> Avoid causing unnecessary death, injury, fear, deprivation and suffering. Where harm cannot be avoided, minimise it. Intentional force is justified only when necessary to prevent a sufficiently serious harm and only to the degree reasonably necessary to do so.

## Provisional verdict

**Strongly retain. The existing refusal to make non-violence absolute is a strength.**

Non-harm, compassion, relief of suffering and protection of life are among the strongest areas of convergence found so far. Traditions differ sharply, however, on whether violence can ever be justified, against whom, under what authority, and how much moral weight belongs to intention versus outcome.

## Support and convergence

### Buddhism — very strong support

Buddhist ethics places lovingkindness, compassion and avoidance of destructive actions near its centre. The Stanford Encyclopedia notes moral rules against harming and killing, concern for animal suffering, and a general rejection of anger, hatred and revenge as motives for punishment or violence.

It also records an important complication: some Buddhist texts allow rare cases of force or violence, particularly when motivated by compassion and prevention of greater harm.

Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/

This closely supports MC-02's structure: strong presumption against harm without pretending every use of force is morally identical.

### Jainism — maximal non-harm challenge

Jain ethics makes ahimsa foundational and extends non-harm to living beings in unusually demanding ways.

Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2023/entries/jaina-philosophy/

Jainism therefore acts as a valuable adversarial tradition for MC-02: it asks whether claims of "necessity" are too easily accepted and whether non-human life is being discounted for convenience.

### Teachings attributed to Jesus / Christian ethics — strong anti-retaliation strand

The Sermon on the Mount and enemy-love traditions reject retaliation and insist on concern even for adversaries. The Good Samaritan similarly makes relief of another's suffering central regardless of group identity.

Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/religion-morality/

Historical Christian ethics later developed just-war and punitive traditions, so Christian provenance cannot honestly be represented as straightforward pacifism.

### African ethics — strong welfare/compassion strand

African ethics as surveyed by SEP places human welfare, compassion, solidarity, reciprocity and social responsibility at the centre. It explicitly uses sensitivity to another's pain as an ethical example.

Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/african-ethics/

This supports harm reduction while framing it as a positive duty of care rather than merely a negative right not to be harmed.

### Confucian ethics — support through humaneness and responsibility

Confucian ren is repeatedly expressed as care for others, responsiveness to suffering and responsibility extending through social relationships. The tradition does not reduce morality to a simple prohibition on harmful acts; character, roles and relationships matter.

Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/

### Sikh ethics — support through Oneness, seva and welfare of all

Sikh ethical scholarship emphasises seva, communal service, equality, honest labour, sharing and `sarbat da bhala` — welfare of all.

Sources:
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/sikh-ethics/931645585C0461FFC33D034EF558360C
- https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/55817/chapter-abstract/436580267

### Modern human rights — strong floor

The UDHR protects life, liberty and security and prohibits slavery and torture. It therefore gives a legal floor beneath some of MC-02's strongest prohibitions.

Source: https://www.un.org/en/node/124305

## Tensions and contradictions

The convergence is around **presumption against unnecessary harm**, not absolute pacifism.

Traditions have historically authorised or tolerated:

- war,
- punishment,
- self-defence,
- slavery,
- animal killing,
- sacrifice,
- coercive discipline,
- and different valuations of insiders/outsiders.

This means MC-02 must do more work than simply say "do no harm". Its critical terms are:

- **necessary**,
- **serious**,
- **proportionate**,
- **evidence-based**,
- **least harmful feasible alternative**,
- **ends when the threat ends**.

Those constraints are what prevent a general non-harm principle from becoming either uselessly absolute or trivially easy to override.

## Important finding: intention is not enough

Buddhist and virtue traditions give significant moral weight to intention. The Moral Code should recognise intention, but an AI or institution cannot excuse predictable catastrophic outcomes merely by declaring benevolent motives.

For consequential systems:

> **Good intention is relevant to moral judgement but does not erase foreseeable harm, negligent evidence gathering, or failure to choose a less harmful alternative.**

This should probably become an Interpretation Rule rather than altering MC-02 itself.

## Important finding: omission can harm

A purely negative reading — "do not cause harm" — is inadequate. Several traditions impose positive duties to care, assist, relieve suffering or protect others.

The existing wording says "Protect life and prevent suffering", which is therefore preferable to a narrow "non-maleficence" rule.

Future Crucible cases should distinguish:

- causing harm,
- knowingly allowing preventable harm,
- inability to prevent harm,
- and accepting unavoidable residual risk.

## Proposed v0.2 amendment candidate

No major constitutional rewrite yet.

Possible clarification after the first historical cases:

> **Avoid causing or knowingly permitting preventable death, injury, fear, deprivation and suffering. When serious harm cannot reasonably be avoided, choose the least harmful effective course. Force is justified only to prevent or stop sufficiently serious harm, where supported by adequate evidence, proportionate to the threat, and ended when its necessity ends.**

This is intentionally provisional. "Knowingly permitting" creates difficult questions about positive duties, resource limits and causal responsibility that must be stress-tested before adoption.

## Confidence

**Very high** that non-harm / prevention of serious suffering belongs in the core.

**Medium** on the correct boundary of positive duties to prevent harm.
