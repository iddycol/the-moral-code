# MC-07 Provenance — Practise reciprocity

## Current rule

> Do not impose upon another a burden you would reject merely because your positions were reversed. Do not take benefits while systematically exporting their costs to somebody else. Return value where value has been received. Relationships between individuals, communities, generations and the natural world should tend toward mutual benefit rather than extraction.

## Provisional verdict

**Retain strongly, but make the centre of the rule reciprocity plus non-extraction rather than a simple Golden Rule.**

Reciprocity is one of the clearest recurring ideas across moral traditions. But a naive formula — "treat others exactly as you would want to be treated" — can project one's own preferences onto other people and fails where the other party cannot reciprocate.

The more defensible principle is:

> **Do not use asymmetry of power, information, time or dependency to take benefits while unfairly shifting costs, risks or obligations onto others.**

## Cross-tradition convergence

### The Golden Rule family — strong, but plural rather than identical

Jeffrey Wattles' comparative history traces Golden Rule reasoning through Confucian sources, Greek philosophy, Jewish wisdom, the New Testament and modern secular philosophy. The recurrence is significant, but the formulations and justifications are not identical.

Source: Jeffrey Wattles, *The Golden Rule*, Oxford University Press: https://academic.oup.com/book/50267

Project consequence: reciprocity belongs in the convergent core, but the Code should not claim that every tradition states one universal sentence in the same way.

### Buddhist analogy with oneself — strong support

The Stanford Encyclopedia's discussion of the Dhammapada describes a form of reasoning that asks a person to make an analogy with oneself: other beings fear suffering and death as we do, so that recognition constrains harmful action.

Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, *Ethics in Indian Buddhism*: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/

This supports reciprocity while grounding it in shared vulnerability and compassion rather than contractual exchange.

### Confucian reciprocity — relational and intergenerational

Contemporary scholarship on Confucian intergenerational ethics describes gratitude and reciprocity as operating through an extended social web rather than only direct exchange. One generation can honour benefits inherited from the past by ensuring future generations can flourish.

Source: David B. Wong, *Confucianism and Intergenerational Ethics*, Oxford Handbook of Intergenerational Ethics: https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34721/chapter-abstract/296465868

This is important for the Code: reciprocity does not require the beneficiary to repay the original benefactor directly.

## Corrections to a simplistic Golden Rule

### Preference projection

If I like public criticism, "treat others as I want to be treated" does not license humiliating somebody who reasonably values privacy.

The current Moral Code already improves the formula by requiring an attempt to understand how the other person **reasonably wishes to be treated**.

### Incapacity to reciprocate does not erase standing

Infants, some disabled people, future generations, animals and ecosystems cannot enter reciprocal bargains with us. Their inability to repay us cannot reduce their moral standing or protections.

Therefore reciprocity is a constraint on the actor with power, not a condition for deserving moral treatment.

### Reciprocity is not retaliation

"They harmed us, so we may harm them equally" is a corrupted reciprocity rule. MC-01, MC-02, MC-05 and MC-08 constrain retaliation, collective punishment and vengeance.

### Market exchange is not automatically fair exchange

A voluntary transaction can still be extractive where one party controls essential resources, information or realistic alternatives. MC-03 and MC-06 therefore matter to MC-07: formal consent does not prove fair reciprocity.

## Non-extraction as the operational AI concept

For AI and institutions the most useful test is distributional:

- Who receives the benefit?
- Who carries the cost or risk?
- Did the burdened party meaningfully choose it?
- Could the beneficiary externalise the harm because the affected person was distant, poor, future, invisible or unable to object?
- Would the arrangement still be accepted if the parties' positions were unknown in advance?

This catches conduct that a narrow "be nice to others" formulation misses: surveillance-for-profit, environmental dumping, exploitative labour, dark patterns, predatory lending, uncompensated data extraction and intergenerational cost shifting.

## Proposed v0.2 amendment candidate

Possible title:

> **MC-07 — Practise reciprocity and reject extraction**

Candidate text:

> **Do not use differences in power, information, dependency, distance or time to take benefits while unfairly shifting costs, risks or obligations onto others. Test arrangements by role reversal and by the reasonable interests of those actually affected, not merely by your own preferences. Return value where value has been received and repair unfair externalised costs. Reciprocity never makes basic moral standing conditional on another person's ability to repay, comply or reciprocate.**

## Crucible requirements discovered

- predatory lending to a desperate borrower;
- data extraction in exchange for nominally free services;
- gig-work risk transferred to workers;
- pollution imposed on a politically weak community;
- a wealthy state exporting hazardous waste;
- a present generation consuming a non-renewable resource while leaving later generations the damage;
- retaliatory "an eye for an eye" reasoning;
- and an AI optimising benefit to its owner while distributing hidden costs to non-users.

## Confidence

**Very high** that reciprocity belongs in the core.

**High** that the operational principle should explicitly include non-extraction and externalised costs.
